\subsection*{Abstract}

\note{TO DO: the final paragraph states the result of the calibrated comparison and is written once
Section~\ref{sec:calibrated_comparison} has been read.}

Comparing clinical trial designs is not straightforward when they differ in sample size,
power and type one error at the same time, and it becomes harder still when the design is
adaptive and the sample size is itself random. This thesis studies Bayesian two arm designs
with a Normal endpoint that borrow historical information on the control arm, and evaluates
them on a common per patient scale.

Borrowing is carried out with meta analytic predictive priors and their robust mixture
extensions. Designs are assessed through average operating characteristics, which separate
the analysis prior used to form the posterior from the design prior used to describe the
plausible true states of nature. Alongside the average type one error, we define an average
power, the counterpart appropriate to control arm borrowing, since classical assurance averages
over uncertainty in the treatment effect rather than over the control parameter. Closed form
expressions for both metrics are derived under Normal priors, together with three properties of
the averaged quantities. Average metrics lie closer to one half than their conditional
counterparts, the average type one error is controlled exactly at the nominal level when the
design and analysis priors coincide, and it degenerates to one half when the design prior
becomes vague while borrowing remains active. Sample sizes are obtained in closed form for a
single Normal analysis prior and by numerical inversion for mixtures, where no closed form
exists.

Designs are then compared through the experimental unit information index, which normalises
the diagnostic odds ratio by the number of patients a design requires and therefore places
designs of different size on the same footing. The framework is developed for fixed designs
under both a single criterion and a dual criterion, and is then extended to group sequential
designs with one and with two interim analyses. Thresholds and sample sizes are calibrated
by simulation, using common random numbers so that the average type one error becomes a
smooth monotone function of the threshold and can be inverted by root finding, with the
threshold recalibrated at every candidate sample size.

Two findings delimit how the index should be used. Under a design prior that conflicts with the
analysis prior, the index barely moves even though power and type one error move substantially,
because it is a ratio of odds and the two shifts largely cancel. A design that is badly optimistic
and one that is badly underpowered can therefore reach nearly the same value, so sensitivity to
prior conflict is better assessed through the average operating characteristics themselves. Under
correctly specified priors the index does discriminate. Every sequential design considered lies
above its fixed counterpart, futility stopping raises it further while leaving the error rates
almost untouched, and the two criteria cross, with the dual criterion ahead for effects moderately
above the decision value and the single criterion ahead for large ones. Adding interim analyses
raises the index but inflates the average type one error, which is why designs are finally compared
after calibration to common error rates rather than to a common threshold.
